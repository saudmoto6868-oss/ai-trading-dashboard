Combination Corrections
=======================

Combination corrections are compound corrective structures in Elliott wave analysis, built by joining two or three simpler corrections with intervening connector waves labeled X. A double three is labeled W-X-Y, and the rarer triple three is labeled W-X-Y-X-Z, which is why traders often call them WXY and WXYXZ patterns. Combination Corrections, also known as double/triple three, WXY/WXYXZ, are Elliott & Harmonics concepts.

What are combination corrections?
---------------------------------

Combination corrections are compound corrective structures in Elliott wave analysis, built by joining two or three simpler corrections with intervening connector waves labeled X. A double three is labeled W-X-Y, and the rarer triple three is labeled W-X-Y-X-Z, which is why traders often call them WXY and WXYXZ patterns. Each component (W, Y, and Z) is itself a complete correction, typically a [zigzag](/library/concept/zigzag/), a flat, or, in the final position only, a triangle.

They exist because markets frequently need more time to correct than a single three-wave structure provides. When a first correction ends without having consumed enough time or retraced enough price relative to what the larger trend requires, the market extends the pause by tacking on another corrective unit via an X wave. The result usually travels mostly sideways: combinations tend to flatten and stretch a correction rather than deepen it.

Traders care because combinations are among the most common reasons wave counts go wrong. An analyst who counts a completed flat and positions for the next corrective wave to be over can be caught when the market delivers an X wave and a second corrective unit instead. Recognizing that a correction may be compound keeps expectations honest: sideways, choppy, overlapping price action that keeps invalidating simple counts is often a combination in progress.

How to identify a combination correction
----------------------------------------

Combinations are usually identified progressively, as simpler counts fail, rather than predicted in advance.

1.  1Start with a completed simple correction (a zigzag or flat) that seems too brief or too shallow for its position in the larger trend.
2.  2Watch for a partial retracement of that correction, the X wave, which is itself a three-wave structure and often deep and choppy.
3.  3Expect the next component (Y) to be a different type than W in many cases; alternation between components is a common guideline.
4.  4The whole structure typically drifts sideways with heavy overlap; a strongly trending correction is more likely a single zigzag or an impulse.
5.  5If a triangle appears, it usually occupies the final position (Y of a double or Z of a triple), after which the larger trend tends to resume.

How traders use it
------------------

*   As a fallback count: when a simple correction is followed by more corrective action instead of a trend resumption, relabeling as W-X-Y preserves a coherent count without forcing an impulse where none exists.
*   For patience in ranges: expecting a combination tells a trader the chop may last considerably longer than a single ABC, discouraging premature breakout entries.
*   To frame invalidation: under standard conventions the combination should stay corrective in character; a clear five-wave move against the larger trend argues for a reversal count instead.
*   With honest limits: combinations are flexible enough to fit almost any sideways mess after the fact. Their forecasting value is modest, and most practitioners use them to manage expectations rather than to generate precise entries.
*   Alongside the wave degree hierarchy: each component must be one degree below the combination itself, which helps keep sprawling sideways counts internally consistent.

Combinations vs other corrective structures
-------------------------------------------

[Flat](/library/concept/flat/): A flat is a single 3-3-5 corrective unit. A combination strings two or three complete corrective units together with X-wave connectors, producing a longer, usually more sideways structure.

[Triangle](/library/concept/triangle/): A triangle is one converging or expanding five-legged structure. In a combination a triangle may appear only as the final component, so a triangle mid-structure argues against the combination label.

[Corrective Wave](/library/concept/corrective-wave/): Corrective wave is the umbrella category. Combinations are the compound branch of it, used when a single zigzag, flat, or triangle cannot account for the observed structure.

Combination Corrections FAQ
---------------------------

### What do W, X, Y, and Z actually stand for?

They are just labels for the successive corrective components (W, Y, Z) and the connectors between them (X). Each of W, Y, and Z is a complete simple correction in its own right.

### How common are triple threes?

Rare. Most analysts encounter double threes with some regularity, while WXYXZ counts are unusual enough that many treat a proposed triple three as a sign the count may be wrong.

### Can a combination be predicted in advance?

Rarely with confidence. Combinations are usually recognized in progress, after a simple count fails. Their main practical value is explaining extended sideways action, not forecasting it.

### Can two zigzags form a combination?

A double zigzag also uses W-X-Y labels, but standard convention classifies it separately from combinations, which are usually described as containing at most one zigzag component. The distinction matters because double zigzags move sharply against the trend while combinations tend toward sideways action.

Gann Theory
===========

Introduction
------------

Few figures in technical analysis are as legendary — or as divisive — as William Delbert Gann. A trader and author active in the first half of the 20th century, Gann claimed that markets were governed by natural laws of geometry, mathematics and time, and he built an intricate system of angles, squares and cycles to exploit them. His admirers regard him as a master; his critics regard much of his work as unfalsifiable mysticism. Both camps agree on one thing: his ideas have left a lasting mark on how traders think about the relationship between **price and time**.

This lesson presents Gann's most enduring and usable concepts — angles, the fan, and the squaring of price and time — while being candid about what is testable and what is not. The goal is not to sell you on Gann, but to help you understand a framework you will inevitably encounter, and to extract the parts that stand on solid analytical ground.

Quick Definition
----------------

> **Gann Theory** is a geometric approach to markets developed by W.D. Gann, built on the premise that **price and time are proportionally linked**. It uses angles (notably the 1×1 line), fans and the "squaring" of price and time to identify trend strength, [support](/glossary/support), [resistance](/glossary/resistance) and potential turning points.

Price and Time as One
---------------------

The idea that sets Gann apart is his insistence that **time matters as much as price**. Most tools focus on price alone — how high, how low, what level. Gann argued that the _rate_ at which price moves through time carries the real information: a market rising steeply is behaving differently from one drifting up slowly, even if both reach the same level. To make this visible, he plotted **angles** that advance a fixed amount of price for a fixed amount of time, turning the price-time relationship into geometry you can see on the chart.

The most important of these is the **1×1 line** — one unit of price per one unit of time, drawn at 45 degrees on a properly scaled chart. Gann treated it as the line of a _balanced_ trend.

Gann Angles and the Fan
-----------------------

From a significant high or low, Gann drew not one line but a family of them — the **Gann fan**. Each line represents a different price-time ratio: the balanced **1×1**, steeper angles like **2×1** and **4×1** (price moving faster than time), and shallower ones like **1×2** and **1×4** (time outpacing price). Together they fan out across the chart.

The fan is read as a hierarchy of **dynamic support and resistance**. In an uptrend, as long as price holds above the 1×1 line, the trend is considered strong. If price breaks below 1×1, Gann expected it to fall to the next shallower angle (1×2), and so on — each angle acting as the next line of defence. The angles thus give a _graduated_ read of trend health rather than a single yes/no level. Crucially, because the angles depend on how the chart's price axis is scaled against its time axis, two analysts using different scales will draw different fans — a genuine weakness we [return](/glossary/return) to below.

Squaring Price and Time
-----------------------

Gann's most mystical-sounding idea is also one of his most characteristic: the **squaring of price and time**. He believed markets reach important turning points when a quantity of price movement comes into balance with a quantity of elapsed time — when, loosely speaking, the "amount" of price and the "amount" of time become proportional or equal on his scale. A market that has risen a certain number of points might, in his framework, be vulnerable to [reversal](/glossary/reversal) when a corresponding number of time units has passed.

Related tools — the **Square of Nine**, a spiral of numbers Gann used to relate prices and dates, and his various **squares** — extend this idea into a system for projecting where and _when_ turns might occur. These constructions are where Gann is at his most esoteric and least testable, and where healthy scepticism is most warranted. The underlying intuition, however — that both the _extent_ and the _[duration](/glossary/duration)_ of a move matter — is sound and echoes ideas you have met in cycles and Elliott Wave.

Time Cycles
-----------

Finally, Gann placed enormous weight on **time cycles** — recurring intervals at which he believed markets were prone to change direction. He studied anniversary dates of major highs and lows, and divisions of the year and larger periods, looking for temporal rhythm in the same spirit that other analysts look for price patterns. Stripped of its more speculative trappings, this is simply the observation — shared with cycle theory — that _when_ a market has been trending for a characteristic length of time, the odds of a change rise. The disciplined takeaway is to watch time, not only price, when judging the maturity of a move.

Real-World Application
----------------------

A trader borrowing selectively from Gann might anchor a fan to a clear, significant pivot low and use the 1×1 line as a _trend-health gauge_: while price rides above it, they give the uptrend the benefit of the doubt; when price decisively loses the 1×1, they treat it as an early warning to tighten risk, watching the next angle down as potential support. They might also note _how long_ a move has run, not just how far, growing more alert to reversal as both the price extension and the elapsed time mature together. Used this way — as one confirming input among several, on a consistently scaled chart — Gann's angles add a disciplined price-_and_\-time dimension. The trader avoids the trap of treating a fan as a precise oracle, and ignores the astrological fringe entirely.

Risks & Limitations
-------------------

*   **Scale-dependent.** Gann angles change with the chart's price-to-time scaling; there is no single "correct" scale, so different analysts draw different fans from the same data.
*   **Hard to test.** Much of Gann's system resists rigorous statistical validation, and some of it (astrology, numerology) has no accepted mechanism at all.
*   **Complexity and opacity.** Gann's original writings are famously cryptic; a great deal of interpretation — and mythology — surrounds them.
*   **Curve-fitting risk.** With enough angles, squares and cycles available, it is easy to find one that "explains" any past move without predictive value.
*   **Best used selectively.** The defensible core (price-time balance, the 1×1 as a trend gauge, time-aware analysis) is useful; the esoteric extensions demand heavy scepticism.

Common Misconceptions
---------------------

*   **"Gann angles are just trendlines."** They are anchored to a fixed _price-time ratio_, not drawn between two price points — so they behave differently as time passes.
*   **"The 1×1 is always literally 45 degrees."** Only on a chart scaled so one price unit equals one time unit; change the scale and the visual angle changes.
*   **"Gann could predict exact tops and bottoms by date."** This is the legend; in practice his time methods are imprecise and unproven, and should be treated as odds-shifters, not forecasts.
*   **"You must accept the astrology to use Gann."** You can adopt the geometric, price-time core while discarding the esoteric material — many practitioners do exactly that.

Key Takeaways
-------------

*   **Gann Theory** rests on the premise that **price and time are geometrically linked** and should be studied together.
*   The **1×1 line** (one price unit per time unit, 45° on a scaled chart) is Gann's balance line; holding above it signals strength, losing it signals weakening.
*   The **Gann fan** provides graduated dynamic support/resistance through steeper and shallower angles around the 1×1.
*   **Squaring price and time** and **time cycles** extend the idea that a move's _duration_ matters as much as its extent — intuitive at the core, esoteric at the fringe.
*   Gann is genuinely **controversial**: scale-dependent, hard to test, and mixed with numerology — valuable when used selectively and sceptically, dangerous when treated as an oracle.

Frequently asked questions
--------------------------

What is the 1×1 line in Gann Theory?

The 1×1 line is a 45-degree angle that advances one unit of price per one unit of time on a properly scaled chart. Gann treated it as the line of a balanced trend, where price holding above it signals trend strength, and breaking below it suggests weakening momentum.

How does a Gann fan work as support and resistance?

A Gann fan consists of multiple angles (like 2×1, 1×1, and 1×2) drawn from a significant high or low, creating a hierarchy of dynamic support and resistance levels. In an uptrend, price is expected to hold above the 1×1 line; if it breaks that level, it may fall to the next shallower angle, with each angle acting as the next line of defense.

Why does Gann Theory emphasize both price and time?

Gann argued that the rate at which price moves through time—not just the price level itself—carries the real information about market behavior. A market rising steeply behaves differently from one drifting up slowly even if both reach the same price level, and this rate is captured by plotting angles that represent fixed price-to-time ratios.

What is the main weakness of Gann angles?

Gann angles are scale-dependent, meaning that two analysts using different price-axis-to-time-axis scales will draw different fans from the same pivot point. This is a genuine analytical weakness because the same market event can produce different angle readings depending on chart scaling choices.

How is the squaring of price and time used in Gann Theory?

Gann believed markets reach important turning points when a quantity of price movement comes into balance with elapsed time—that is, when the proportional amount of price and amount of time become equal on his scale. Tools like the Square of Nine and various squares extend this idea to project where and when reversals might occur, though these are considered among Gann's most esoteric and least testable concepts.

W D Gann
========

![William Delbert Gann](https://cyclesresearchinstitute.org/images/gann.jpg)

William Delbert Gann (1878-1955)
--------------------------------

**See [Writings Index](https://cyclesresearchinstitute.org/subjects/cycles-economy/gann/)**

W D Gann is a legendary name in the world of stock and commodity trading. Gann was supposedly one of the most successful stock and commodity traders that ever lived. Born in Lufkin, Texas on June 6, 1878, W.D. Gann began trading in the early 1900’s, and in 1908 moved to New York City to open his own brokerage firm, Gann & Company. Using his own style of technical analysis, W.D. Gann was reported to have taken more than 50 million dollars in profits out of the markets.

Gann based his trading methods on time and price analysis. This made it possible for Mr. Gann to determine not only when a trend change was  
imminent, but also what the best price would be to enter, or exit that market. So accurate were Mr. Gann’s techniques that in the in the  
presence of representatives of a major financial publication, he made 286 trades in a period of 25 market days. Of these, 264 trades were  
profitable.

W.D. Gann’s use of Natural Law and geometric proportions based on the circle, square, and triangle are as effective today in the stock and  
commodities markets as they were while he was alive.

A number of files about Gann’s techniques are available for [downloading](https://cyclesresearchinstitute.org/files/Gann-Master-Chart.rar). These are mostly **zip** files of **pdf** files.

A Summary of W.D. Gann’s Techniques of Analysis and Trading

Psychological Framework

Master yourself  
\* Do not overtrade  
\* See if your trade is based on hope or logic and systems developed by you

Trading strategies  
Have different strategies for the four situations:  
\* Bull market  
\* Bull market top i.e. reversal from bull to bear market phase  
\* Bear market  
\* Reversal phase from bear to bull market

Importance of number 3  
Majority of moves will generally occur in time period of three – days, weeks or months. Never trade in the direction of the trend on its third day.

Tops, bottoms and consolidations  
\* Tops usually take time to form. Spike tops are less common compared to spike bottoms. Tops are marked by extreme movements in medium and small stocks. They will rise by even 20% in a day. These are called blow offs. Because of this short-selling on extreme top is risky.  
\* Divergences will appear at the top but they cannot be used for timing the trade. Time cycles shall indicate when the actual reversal will start.  
\* In bull market watch for a correction which is greater in both price and time than the previous corrections in the move up. (Opposite in the downmoves).  
\* Highest probability of support is that the corrections in the uptrend will all be very close to equal.  
\* Swing objectives – add the range to move to the top of that move to find out the target for the next upmove or reverse in the bear market.  
\* Square of numbers and 50% of the difference between those squares are significant support and resistance, but cannot be traded by themselves.

Gann says that there can be nine mathematical proofs of any point of resistance  
1\. Angles from top and bottoms  
2\. Angles running horizontally i.e. the previous tops and bottoms  
3\. Time cycles (vertical angles) (Press a short sale if there are three or four days  
of sideways movement after a high day and this is followed by a down day with  
high volume where low is lower than the low of the sideways movement and  
when this coincides with expiry of time cycles)  
4\. Crossing of important angles originating at zero  
5\. Crossing or coming together of angles from double or triple tops or bottoms  
6\. Crossing of double or triple tops or bottoms  
7\. Past resistance/ support  
8\. Volume of sales  
9\. Squaring of time and price.

Weak stocks will generally not rally until either a test of the first bottom or a higher bottom is made by the market. (That is why AD line is a lagging indicator and generally moves up in the third wave)The third move trying to break the consolidation top/bottom is the most important. If it fails, a fast move in the other direction may be expected.

False breakouts from consolidation result in very fast moves. False breakout occurs when a move outside the consolidation zone fails to sustain in the following week and where the price has not gone beyond three points above the top. These false moves start with high momentum.

A breakout from a three-four day consolidation in a very narrow range results in sharp three day move.

Faster moves start from third of fourth higher bottom. It will be strong move if there is space between the third or fourth bottom and the previous top.

Trend and trend following techniques: In fast advancing markets, in the last stage of the campaign, reactions get smaller as stocks work to higher level, until the final run has ended. Then comes a sharp reaction and a reversal in the trend. Same happens in the bear market. Once you are convinced that a trend is in force, do not wait too long to go with the trade. Early in the trend buy/sell a stock which is already strong/weak. Fast moves generally come from bear market bottoms. These moves usually run three weeks up, then move sideways three to five more weeks, and then accelerate followed by another sideways movement. Under fast moves the first signal to trend change is overbalance i.e. reaction gets larger compared to the earlier ones, specially in the fifth wave. Watch the changes in momentum of price – is the market/stock gaining less points in more time? If the market is trending up, then it should go up more time than it goes down. And vice versa.

Any reversal pattern should be seen in conjunction with the time cycles. Do not pay attention to the financial press.

Use simple trading filter of not entering the market on the third day of the move.

The Cycle of years : Seasonality

Watch for significant days in solar year – Dec. 22, March 21, June 22, Sep 21/23 etc. and days on important angles from these days e.g. 15 days from Dec. 22 i.e. Jan 5-6, Feb 5, May 6, July 7, August 8 etc.

Important count of days: Significant changes in trend may take place on the following days from the significant highs/ lows – 30, 45, 60, 90, 135, 150, 180, 210, 225, 315, 330 and 360.

These are calendar day counts: Trading day counts are 11, 22, 33, 45, 56, 67, 78, 90, 101, 112, 123, 135, 146, 157, 168 and 180. True understanding of cycles are obtained from the calendar days.

Important count of weeks: 13, 26, 39, 45, 52, 78. 7 week period is considered as death zone. Important count of months – 6, 12, 144.

Geometric Charts, angles and price squares: 365 days is an important cycle of one year. In a circle there are 360 degrees which very nearly correspond to this cycle. In other words, one day is equal to one degree of the circle that the earth makes around the sun. Hence the significance of the important divisions of the circle (into angles) on the chart. These angles are 45, 90, 120, 135, 180, 225, 240, 270, 315 and 360.

Dividing a line parallel to the 90 degree division of the circle we get a square. Divisions of this square gives important angles on the charts.

There are two kinds of cycles: Time cycle or natural cycles and cycles derived from the significant prices. And these cycles will have important divisions on 1/8, 1/4, 1/3, 3/8, 1/2, 5/8, 2/3, 3/4 and 7/8. Thus the 30 year time cycle will be divided into important probable turning points as follows:

1/8 – 3.75 years

The square of range can be made down from top or up from low. In a square of high at say 60, drawn on daily graph will have its corners at the following four points – 1) at the price (at 60) 2) at the price (at 60) 60 days away in future i.e. 60 on price axis 60 days

If the price moves down one point each day the price will reach to the point 4, i.e. 0 on the 60th day. This action is called squaring off of the price. The angle of fall will be 45% on the square. This is also referred to as 1×1 angle i.e. fall of one unit in one day. In same fashion we can draw angle 2×1 i.e. fall of two units in one day and so on. The most significant angles are 2×1, 1×1 and 1×2. These angles are drawn from point 1, 3 and the mid-point between 1 and 3 and the mid-point on 2 and 4.

Crossing over of the angles drawn on the square are considered probable turning points. Angles from 50% mark should always be seen.

When the price breaks below 45% angle line it signifies a weak position and indicates a decline to the next angle. If it again crosses the 45% angle it is said to have regained its strength. At the crossover of these angles distance from the base i.e. the day of high/ low is important. Larger the distance, more powerful the trend is likely to be.

1/2 is the most important level. This is the centre of gravity. If the price falls below this level and bounces back to touch this level again, on the first such occasion it is good set up to shortsell. If the price comes to 50% of high and 50% in time, it may be a high probability buy which may result in 3 months fast move up on the weekly chart.

The third time against any support or resistance zone is the dangerous time.  
The 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 7th, 9th and 12th squares are the significant squares of lows but

Overview of Sources for W D Gann’s Lessons

The main thrust of this overview is to provide information regarding the various sources of W.D. Gann’s original lessons. It will not include second-generation “How I Cracked Gann”-type books, or courses. That’s another story…

IMO the single best Internet resource for finding out most things (that are publicly available) relating to W.D. Gann is the web site run by Bonnie Lee Hill. She has put together a formidable resource. Most, if not all, of the links presented here should be readily found on her site. URL: http://www.webspace4me.net/~blhill/pages/w.d.gann.html

Newbies should understand that, to my knowledge, Gann never published a singular Master Stock Course, or a singular Master Commodities Course. Rather, he published a string of lessons – many in the form of letters to students – over a period of many years (i.e. 1931-1955). This is separate to his Annual Forecasts of the 1920s. It appears that this method of publishing (letters) was not uncommon during that era. There is good evidence to suggest that W.D. compiled many of these lessons into courses that he subsequently marketed at different times, prior to his death in 1955. My research to date indicates that the notion of a singular Stock Market Course and a singular Commodities Course is something that has evolved since the 1970s.

The purpose of this background is to provide newbies with some understanding as to why the commercial vendors have slight differences in the original Gann material that they provide – and there are differences. When one understands that their courses are mainly a collection of letters/lessons created over a period of 25-odd years, then it’s easy to understand the potential for differences in their educational material – depending on how well each vendor could source the original lessons. What is an Impulse?
-------------------

An impulse is the five-wave engine of Elliott wave theory: a move in the direction of the next larger trend that subdivides 1-2-3-4-5, with waves 1, 3, and 5 advancing and waves 2 and 4 retracing. Ralph Nelson Elliott bound the form with three hard rules: wave 2 never retraces all of wave 1, wave 3 is never the shortest advancing wave, and wave 4 does not overlap wave 1's price territory. Waves 1, 3, and 5 are themselves motive waves that subdivide into smaller fives, the [fractal nesting](/library/concept/fractal-nesting/) that lets one count run across degrees.

The impulse is the theory's trend statement, which is why it matters beyond labeling. A completed five-wave advance implies the larger degree points up and that the following corrective wave is countertrend, something to position into rather than flee. Just as important, the [hard rules](/library/concept/elliott-hard-rules/) make every impulse label falsifiable: each count carries exact prices where it dies, which separates wave work from free-form pattern reading. In practice, one of the three advancing waves typically extends, running far beyond the other two.

How to identify an impulse
--------------------------

An impulse is verified by counting five swings and then trying to break the count against the rules.

1.  1Find five swings in the trend direction: three advances (1, 3, 5) separated by two retracements (2 and 4), each advance making clear directional progress.
2.  2Test the rules: wave 2 must hold above the start of wave 1, wave 3 must not be the shortest advance, and wave 4 must stay out of wave 1's price range.
3.  3Check subdivision: waves 1, 3, and 5 should themselves resemble smaller five-wave moves, while waves 2 and 4 subdivide in threes.
4.  4Cross-check proportions: typical impulses show one extended advancing wave and retracements near common fib ratios; if every wave needs an unusual proportion, suspect the label.

How traders use it
------------------

*   As directional bias: a clean five-wave advance off a low is read as evidence the larger trend has turned up, so subsequent three-wave pullbacks are treated as entry opportunities rather than reversals.
*   As a falsifiable count: each rule doubles as an invalidation level. A presumed wave 2 that trades below the start of wave 1, or a wave 4 that trades into wave 1's range, kills the label and often anchors the stop.
*   For targets: fib wave relationships project wave 3 and wave 5 objectives from the earlier waves, most commonly 1.618 times wave 1 for wave 3.
*   For exhaustion: a wave 5 that makes a new price extreme on weaker momentum than wave 3 is the classic divergence context used to anticipate the coming correction.

Impulse vs related concepts
---------------------------

[Motive Wave](/library/concept/motive-wave/): Motive is the umbrella term for waves that travel with the larger trend. Every impulse is motive, but so are diagonals, wedge-shaped forms that permit the wave overlap an impulse forbids. A wave is an impulse only when it obeys all the impulse rules.

[Impulse Leg](/library/concept/impulse-leg/): An impulse leg is the generic market-structure term for any fast directional swing, with no wave count implied. Elliott's impulse is a strict five-wave grammar with rules; an impulse leg only needs speed and direction.

[Corrective Wave](/library/concept/corrective-wave/): The counterpart: corrective waves move against the one-larger trend and subdivide in threes. If a countertrend move develops five clean waves, Elliott practice re-labels it as wave A of a larger correction or the first wave of a new trend, because an impulse-shaped five is never a whole correction by itself.

Impulse FAQ
-----------

### What are the three rules of an impulse wave?

Wave 2 may not retrace more than 100% of wave 1, wave 3 may never be the shortest among waves 1, 3, and 5, and wave 4 may not enter wave 1's price territory. The overlap rule is applied strictly in cash markets, though some practitioners tolerate marginal overlap in leveraged or thin markets. Break a rule and the count is wrong by definition.

### Does a completed impulse guarantee the trend continues?

No. The standard expectation is a corrective phase and then, provided the five did not complete a pattern at one larger degree, at least one more motive wave in the same direction, but wave counts are probabilistic and get re-labeled in real time. What a completed five gives you is a framework: defined invalidation levels and a map of what should happen next, not a certainty.

### Which wave of an impulse usually extends?

One of waves 1, 3, or 5 typically extends, meaning it runs far longer than the other two and subdivides visibly. Elliott literature treats third-wave extensions as the most common in stock indices, with fifth-wave extensions more frequent in commodities. Treat that as a guideline from the source texts, not a statistical law.

Zigzag
======

A zigzag is the sharpest of the Elliott corrective structures: a three-wave A-B-C move against the prevailing trend in which wave A subdivides into five, wave B into three, and wave C into five, the 5-3-5 form. Zigzag, also known as double/triple zigzag, is a Elliott & Harmonics concept.

What is a zigzag?
-----------------

A zigzag is the sharpest of the Elliott corrective structures: a three-wave A-B-C move against the prevailing trend in which wave A subdivides into five, wave B into three, and wave C into five, the 5-3-5 form. Wave B stays well short of the start of wave A, typically retracing between roughly 0.382 and 0.79 of it in the Frost and Prechter guideline, and wave C typically travels beyond the end of wave A. The result looks like a deep, direct countertrend swing rather than the sideways drift of other [corrective waves](/library/concept/corrective-wave/).

Zigzags exist in the grammar because corrections come in two broad temperaments, sharp and sideways, and the zigzag is the sharp one. It commonly appears in wave 2 positions, where the crowd still believes in the old trend and sells (or buys) hard against the new one. When one zigzag does not travel far enough, the market can chain them into a double or triple zigzag, two or three zigzags linked by intervening three-wave structures labeled X, which is what the double/triple zigzag alias refers to.

Traders care about zigzags for a practical reason: wave C is a five-wave [impulse](/library/concept/impulse/)\-form leg with measurable relationships. C frequently approaches equality with A, one of the standard [Fib wave relationships](/library/concept/fib-wave-relationships/), which is the same geometry harmonic traders measure as an [AB=CD®](/library/concept/abcd/). A completed zigzag with C near equality, landing in the area of a prior fourth wave, is one of the classic places to look for the larger trend to resume under [Elliott Wave Theory](/library/concept/elliott-wave-theory/).

How to identify a zigzag on a chart
-----------------------------------

The 5-3-5 subdivision is the defining test; the outer shape alone is not enough.

1.  1Locate a countertrend move with two distinct legs in the same direction separated by a smaller bounce; the overall look is steep and direct, not sideways.
2.  2Verify wave A subdivides into five waves; a three-wave A points instead toward a flat or triangle interpretation.
3.  3Check that wave B is itself a three and stops well short of the start of A, most often below a 0.79 retracement per the standard guideline; a B that returns near the start of A argues for a flat.
4.  4Confirm wave C subdivides into five and carries beyond the end of A; measure C against A, with equality and 1.618 the common reference ratios.
5.  5If price fails to resume the prior trend after C, watch for an X wave and a second zigzag: doubles and triples extend the correction when the first zigzag falls short in price or time.

How traders use it
------------------

*   To anticipate trend resumption: a completed 5-3-5 with C near equality with A gives a measurable zone to watch for the larger trend to reassert, with invalidation beyond the start of wave A.
*   As a wave-2 template: sharp, deep corrections after a fresh impulse are counted as zigzags first, which sets expectations for a strong third wave if the low of the zigzag holds.
*   For target setting inside the correction: once A and B are in place, C is projected at 1.0 and 1.618 times A, giving levels to stage countertrend exits or trend re-entries in advance.
*   With honest limits: real-time subdivision counting is subjective, and a developing zigzag is often indistinguishable from the first legs of a larger decline; position sizing should reflect that the label can change as bars print.

Zigzag vs related structures
----------------------------

[Flat](/library/concept/flat/): A flat is the sideways 3-3-5 correction: wave B retraces most or all of A. A zigzag is the sharp 5-3-5 with a shallow B; the internal count of wave A is the earliest separator.

[Combination corrections](/library/concept/combination-corrections/): Combinations chain different corrective types with X waves. A double zigzag chains the same type twice, keeping a persistent countertrend slope rather than the sideways character of most combinations.

[ZigZag structure (indicator)](/library/concept/zigzag-structure/): The zigzag indicator filters price into swings above a threshold and has no Elliott meaning. The Elliott zigzag is a specific 5-3-5 corrective form; the two share only a name.

Zigzag FAQ
----------

### What is the difference between a zigzag and a flat correction?

Internal structure and depth. A zigzag is 5-3-5 with wave B retracing well under the start of A, producing a sharp countertrend move; a flat is 3-3-5 with B returning near or beyond the start of A, producing a sideways range. Counting the subdivisions of wave A is the standard early test.

### What is a double or triple zigzag?

Two or three complete zigzags linked by corrective X waves, labeled W-X-Y (and Z for triples). The market builds them when a single zigzag does not achieve enough price or time correction. Each additional zigzag keeps the overall move sloped against the trend rather than flattening out.

### How far does wave C usually travel in a zigzag?

Equality with wave A is the most cited relationship, with 1.618 times A the common alternative in stronger corrections. These are tendencies rather than requirements; the structural requirement is only that C exceeds the end of A in the textbook form.

### Where do zigzags most often appear?

Wave 2 positions are the classic home, since second waves tend to be sharp and deep. Zigzags also form the legs inside larger structures, including the A wave of flats and the sub-waves of triangles, which is why the 5-3-5 unit is worth learning to recognize on sight.

What is a triangle in Elliott wave analysis?
--------------------------------------------

In Elliott wave analysis, a triangle is a five-legged sideways corrective structure, labeled A-B-C-D-E, in which every leg subdivides into three waves. It is a precise grammatical object, distinct from the classical chart-pattern triangle drawn from any converging trendlines: the Elliott triangle demands exactly five contacts, three-wave internals, and a specific set of allowed positions within the larger count, appearing in the wave 4, wave B, or final position of a combination, but never as wave 2 on its own.

The recognized variants describe how the boundary lines behave. In a contracting triangle, the most common form, each leg is shorter than the last and the boundaries converge. In a barrier triangle, one boundary is roughly horizontal while the other slopes toward it. In an expanding triangle, rare and difficult to trade, each leg exceeds the previous one and the boundaries diverge. A running triangle has its B wave exceeding the start of wave A, tilting the structure in the direction of the larger trend, which many analysts read as a sign of trend strength.

Traders care because triangles carry a strong positional message: they appear in the position immediately before the final actionable wave of the larger sequence. A triangle in wave 4 precedes the fifth wave; a triangle in wave B precedes the C wave. The energy compressed during the five overlapping legs tends to be released in a thrust out of the pattern, and the fact that the move after the triangle is typically the last leg of its sequence makes the structure as useful for exits as for entries.

How to identify an Elliott triangle
-----------------------------------

The count of legs and their internal structure separate a genuine triangle from ordinary converging congestion.

1.  1Count exactly five legs, A through E, each subdividing into three waves; a leg that subdivides into five breaks the pattern.
2.  2Draw the A-C and B-D boundary lines and classify the variant: contracting, barrier, expanding, or running by the depth of wave B.
3.  3Check position in the larger count: wave 4, wave B, or the final position of a [combination](/library/concept/combination-corrections/). A standalone wave 2 triangle violates convention.
4.  4Expect volume and volatility to shrink as a contracting triangle matures, and note that wave E frequently undershoots or briefly overshoots the boundary line before the thrust.
5.  5Treat a decisive five-wave move out of the pattern as the thrust, and a return deep into the triangle's body as evidence the count was wrong.

How traders use it
------------------

*   For thrust trades: a completed E wave sets up entry in the direction of the larger trend, with a common measuring convention projecting the triangle's widest part from the breakout area.
*   As a sequence warning: because the post-triangle move is usually the final wave of its degree, traders treat the thrust as a move to take profits into rather than the start of a fresh campaign.
*   For patience during wave 4: recognizing a developing triangle explains weeks of overlapping chop and discourages both premature breakout entries and abandoning the trend position.
*   In alternation logic: a sharp wave 2 raises the odds that wave 4 forms a triangle or other sideways structure, per the Elliott guidelines.
*   With honest limits: real-time E-wave identification is notoriously hard, since every leg looks like a breakout attempt, and expanding triangles in particular resist practical trading.

Elliott triangle vs neighboring concepts
----------------------------------------

[Ascending / Descending / Symmetrical Triangle](/library/concept/ascending-descending-symmetrical-triangle/): The classical patterns need only converging trendlines and can break either way. The Elliott triangle requires five three-wave legs, restricted positions, and implies the thrust direction from the larger count.

[Flat](/library/concept/flat/): A flat has three legs ending in a five-wave C. A triangle has five legs, all threes, and typically compresses volatility far more before releasing it.

[Broadening Formation](/library/concept/broadening-formation/): The classical broadening formation resembles the expanding triangle variant, but the Elliott version adds the five-legs-of-threes requirement and positional constraints the classical pattern lacks.

Triangle FAQ
------------

### Where can a triangle appear in a wave count?

In wave 4 of an impulse, wave B of a correction, or as the final component of a combination. Orthodox convention excludes a standalone triangle in the wave 2 position.

### What is the thrust out of a triangle?

The move that follows wave E, usually in the direction of the larger trend. A common measuring convention projects the triangle's widest width from the breakout, though it is a tendency rather than a rule.

### What is a running triangle?

A variant whose B wave exceeds the start of wave A, tilting the pattern in the trend's direction. It is generally read as evidence of underlying trend strength.

### How is this different from a symmetrical triangle pattern?

The classical pattern is defined by shape alone. The Elliott triangle adds strict internal grammar: five legs, each a three, plus positional rules, which makes it rarer but more informative when valid.

What is a flat?
---------------

A flat is a sideways corrective structure in Elliott wave analysis, subdividing 3-3-5: wave A is a three, wave B is a three that retraces most or all of A, and wave C is a five. The name reflects the shape, since the structure tends to travel sideways rather than sharply against the trend. Flats typically appear where the larger trend is strong, most often in the wave 4 or wave B position, where the market pauses without giving up much ground.

Three variants are distinguished by how far wave B carries. In a regular flat, B retraces roughly to the start of A and C ends slightly beyond A's extreme. In an expanded flat, the most common variant in many markets, B exceeds the start of A and C then travels beyond A's extreme, producing a structure whose edges widen and famously trap traders on both sides. In a running flat, B exceeds the start of A but C fails to reach A's extreme, leaving the whole correction tilted in the direction of the larger trend; it is rare and usually signals unusual trend strength.

Traders care because the flat's B wave is a machine for generating false signals. An expanded flat's B wave makes a new price extreme against the recent swing, looking like trend resumption, right before the five-wave C leg goes the other way. Knowing the 3-3-5 blueprint helps a practitioner treat a three-wave push to marginal new extremes with suspicion rather than chase it, and to expect the C wave that a [corrective wave](/library/concept/corrective-wave/) of this type implies.

How to identify a flat
----------------------

The diagnostic sequence is three up (or down), three back, five to finish, with the B-wave depth determining the variant.

1.  1Locate a correction that begins with a clearly three-wave A leg; a five-wave A argues for a zigzag instead.
2.  2Measure wave B: retracement of at least the large majority of A is required under common conventions, and beyond 100 percent indicates the expanded or running variants.
3.  3Expect wave C to subdivide into five waves and to show more impulsive character than A and B.
4.  4Classify the variant: regular (B near A's start, C slightly beyond A), expanded (B beyond A's start, C beyond A's extreme), or running (B beyond A's start, C short of A's extreme).
5.  5Check context: flats favor wave 4 and wave B positions, and via the guideline of alternation often follow a sharp wave 2 elsewhere in the same impulse.

How traders use it
------------------

*   To avoid B-wave traps: a three-wave push to a marginal new high or low, especially on weak internals, is treated as a possible expanded-flat B rather than confirmed continuation.
*   For C-wave entries: once B completes, traders position for the five-wave C leg, often using Fibonacci extensions of A projected from B (commonly around 1.0 to 1.618) as targets.
*   Through alternation: if wave 2 was a sharp zigzag, Elliott guidelines suggest wave 4 may be a flat or other sideways structure, shaping expectations before the correction unfolds.
*   As a trend-strength read: running flats, where even the C wave cannot reach A's extreme, are taken as evidence of a market so strong the correction could not complete normally. They are rare, and counts relying on them deserve extra skepticism.
*   With honest limits: in real time, a developing flat is hard to distinguish from the start of a reversal or a combination, so most practitioners wait for the C wave's five-wave character before committing.

Flats vs other corrections
--------------------------

[Zigzag](/library/concept/zigzag/): A zigzag subdivides 5-3-5, moves sharply against the trend, and has a shallow B wave. A flat subdivides 3-3-5, moves sideways, and has a deep B wave.

[Triangle](/library/concept/triangle/): A triangle has five legs, each a three, inside converging or diverging boundaries. A flat has only three legs and its final leg is a five, giving it a more decisive ending.

[Combination Corrections](/library/concept/combination-corrections/): A flat is a single corrective unit; combinations chain multiple units together with X waves. A flat that keeps extending sideways may be relabeled as one component of a combination.

Flat FAQ
--------

### What makes an expanded flat so deceptive?

Its B wave exceeds the start of the correction, printing a new extreme that looks like trend continuation. The five-wave C leg that follows moves the other way, often catching both breakout traders and early counter-trend traders.

### How deep should wave B retrace in a flat?

Common convention asks for roughly 90 percent of wave A or more. Well beyond 100 percent points to the expanded variant, which many practitioners consider the most frequent form.

### Where do flats usually appear?

Most often in wave 4 and wave B positions, where the larger trend is strong. Wave 2 corrections are more often sharp zigzags, per the guideline of alternation.

### How do I know a flat is finished?

The most useful evidence is a completed five-wave C leg, often reaching a measured extension of A, followed by an impulsive move in the prior trend's direction. Confirmation always trails the actual low or high.

What are diagonals?
-------------------

Diagonals are the wedge-shaped members of the motive family in Elliott wave analysis: five-wave structures that move in the direction of the one-larger trend but violate the normal impulse rule against overlap, with wave 4 typically entering the territory of wave 1. They come in two positional types. A leading diagonal appears at the start of a move, in the wave 1 position of an impulse or the wave A position of a zigzag. An ending diagonal appears at the end, in the wave 5 position or the C-wave position, and signals exhaustion of the larger move.

Their internal structure is the key diagnostic. In the classic ending diagonal, each of the five waves subdivides into three (a 3-3-3-3-3 structure), and the boundary lines drawn across waves 1-3 and 2-4 converge into a wedge. Leading diagonals may subdivide 5-3-5-3-5 or 3-3-3-3-3 depending on the source. Expanding variants, where the boundary lines diverge, are described in the literature but are rare and viewed skeptically by many practitioners.

Traders care because diagonals carry unusually specific implications. An ending diagonal is one of the few Elliott structures with a strong terminal message: when it completes, the move it ends is often retraced swiftly, frequently back to where the diagonal began. A leading diagonal warns that the new trend is starting hesitantly, so the following wave 2 correction tends to be deep. Both are easy to confuse with the classical rising or falling wedge, which encodes similar exhaustion logic without the wave-count machinery.

How to identify a diagonal
--------------------------

Diagonals are identified by position, converging boundaries, and internal three-wave subdivisions.

1.  1Check the position first: wave 1 or A for a leading diagonal, wave 5 or C for an ending diagonal. A wedge in any other wave position is not a diagonal under standard rules.
2.  2Draw boundary lines across the extremes of waves 1 and 3 and of waves 2 and 4; in the common contracting form they should converge.
3.  3Confirm overlap: wave 4 typically enters wave 1 territory, which would invalidate an ordinary impulse but is characteristic here.
4.  4Inspect subdivisions: threes throughout for an ending diagonal; momentum usually diverges as the pattern grinds toward its apex.
5.  5Watch for the throw-over: a brief push through the outer boundary near completion, often on fading momentum, frequently precedes the reversal.

How traders use it
------------------

*   As reversal setups: a completed ending diagonal, especially after a throw-over and a break back inside the wedge, is traded as a reversal signal with a stop beyond the pattern extreme.
*   To set retracement expectations: because ending diagonals are often retraced fully and quickly, targets near the diagonal's origin are common, though nothing about the pattern makes that outcome certain.
*   To anticipate deep wave 2 pullbacks: after a leading diagonal in wave 1, analysts expect a deep second-wave correction and avoid chasing the initial move.
*   As count protection: recognizing legitimate overlap in a diagonal prevents discarding a valid motive count for violating the hard rules that apply to ordinary impulses.
*   With honest limits: wedges are common and most are not diagonals. Requiring correct position, subdivisions, and converging boundaries filters out many candidates, and even qualified diagonals fail.

Diagonals vs confusable structures
----------------------------------

[Rising / Falling Wedge](/library/concept/rising-falling-wedge/): The classical wedge is defined purely by converging trendlines and volume behavior, with no wave-count requirements. A diagonal is a wedge that additionally satisfies Elliott position and subdivision rules.

[Impulse](/library/concept/impulse/): An impulse forbids wave 4 overlapping wave 1 and subdivides 5-3-5-3-5. A diagonal permits the overlap, converges, and in its ending form subdivides entirely into threes.

[Truncation](/library/concept/truncation/): Both signal terminal weakness in a fifth wave, but truncation is a fifth wave that fails to exceed the third wave's extreme, while an ending diagonal is a full wedge-shaped structure that usually does make new price extremes before reversing.

Diagonals FAQ
-------------

### What is the difference between a leading and an ending diagonal?

Position and meaning. A leading diagonal starts a move (wave 1 or A) and implies a deep correction before the trend continues. An ending diagonal finishes a move (wave 5 or C) and implies reversal.

### Are diagonals bullish or bearish?

Neither inherently. A rising ending diagonal is a bearish exhaustion signal and a falling one is bullish. Direction of the implied reversal depends on the trend the diagonal terminates.

### How reliable is the full retracement after an ending diagonal?

It is a tendency described in the literature, not a rule. Swift retracement toward the pattern's origin happens often enough to inform targets, but no structure removes the need for stops.

### Do expanding diagonals exist?

They appear in some texts, with diverging boundary lines, but they are rare and contested. Many practitioners treat a proposed expanding diagonal as a low-confidence count.
